How it works

Three steps, every time

  1. 1

    Ask for a draft, or paste one in

    Write, reply, rewrite or check. Say who's reading and what you need from them. If a key fact is missing, it asks one question.

  2. 2

    It builds the piece around your point

    The point goes first, then the details that back it up. You choose the methods below. It picks the format: an email, a text, a complaint, a toast or a card.

  3. 3

    It checks every line

    A scan for 36 patterns that read as machine-made or padded, then a read for sentences that could be anyone's. It fixes what it finds before you see the draft.

The methods

Six methods, and why each rewrite works

Minto's pyramid is always on. The rest come in when a piece needs them, and each one changes one thing. Pick a method to see that change, before and after.

Pyramid Principle

Barbara Minto Always on

Use it for: Everything. It's the base every other method sits on.

Why it was rewritten this way

  1. The ask moved from the last sentence to the first. Mark decides how closely to read in the first few seconds, so the thing he has to act on goes there.
  2. “Hectic” went. It described the writer's week, and Mark didn't need it to decide anything.
  3. A date range came in. It makes yes easy, and that's the job of everything under the point.

SCQA

Barbara Minto The opening

Use it for: Explaining a decision or a change to people who need the context first.

Why it was rewritten this way

  1. Situation first: the thing everyone already knows, that it's usually at Mom's.
  2. Then the complication that changes it. The kitchen answers the question everyone would ask, before they ask it.
  3. Then the answer, with a time and a date. “A lot of moving pieces” went, because it told nobody anything.

MECE

Barbara Minto The grouping

Use it for: Plans, options and lists of asks: anything with parts.

Why it was rewritten this way

  1. Every item now sits in exactly one group, and the groups cover everything. That's what MECE means: mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive.
  2. The groups follow one rule, who's doing it, so each person finds their job in one place.
  3. The cabin came up twice and now appears once. The scanner can't see a muddle like this one. The method can.

Inverted Pyramid

Newsroom practice The order

Use it for: News, announcements, changes of plan and recaps.

Why it was rewritten this way

  1. The news is now the first sentence: what, where and when. Anyone who stops reading there still has it.
  2. The rest follows in order of importance, so the most useful detail, where they'll be, comes next.
  3. The backstory went. It mattered more to the people moving than to the people reading.

Writing Without Bullshit

Josh Bernoff The edit

Use it for: Busy readers: a company, an office, a contractor. Bad news, too.

Why it was rewritten this way

  1. The ask and the facts moved into the first two sentences, where a busy reader decides what to do with a message.
  2. Passive voice became who did what: “may have been charged” is now “you charged.”
  3. Hedges and stock phrases came out: “appears,” “possibly,” “at your earliest convenience.” 38 words became 18, and the deadline makes it easy to act on.

Storytelling with Data

Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic The numbers

Use it for: Anything with figures: budgets, bills, comparisons.

Why it was rewritten this way

  1. One big idea up front: it saves money, and here's how much.
  2. Only the numbers that matter, each next to a comparison: the new price beside the old one, the yearly total beside something real.
  3. “A lot cheaper” and “significant” asked the reader to take it on trust. The numbers don't.

Scan your own draft

This is the skill's own scanner, running in your browser. Paste anything you've written and it marks the phrases that make writing sound machine-made, with a fix for each. Nothing you type leaves this page.

Inflation

Vagueness

Gesturing at meaning without a claim. Experts say, a sentence that ends in highlighting something.

Mechanical rhythm

Patterns a reader feels before they can name them. It's not X, it's Y, em dashes, sentences all the same length.

Packaging

Formatting that looks like a chatbot answer. I hope this message finds you well, In summary, bold labels.

The scanner sees patterns, not meaning. After a clean scan, ask of every sentence: could it appear, unchanged, in a thousand other people's version of this piece? If yes, replace it with something only yours could say.

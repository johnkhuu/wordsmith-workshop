Wordsmith Workshop drafts and audits the writing you send as yourself: a note to the contractor, a complaint to the airline, a toast, a card. Every piece starts from Minto's pyramid, and the skill scans every draft for the patterns that make writing read like a chatbot wrote it.

How it works

Five layers, always in this order

A buried point can't be fixed with line edits, so structure comes first. Tells come last. Most of them disappear once the structure is right and the details are specific, and the audit catches the rest.

  1. 1

    Structure Always on

    Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle. The skill settles the governing thought and the points under it before writing any prose.

  2. 2

    Methods You pick

    Up to five extra methods per piece. Each one controls a different part of the writing, so they stack without making it longer.

  3. 3

    Format Inferred

    Standard, Brief, Persuade, Story or Letter, chosen from the request and named at the end so you can switch.

  4. 4

    Craft and voice Your voice

    Ten habits of a strong copywriter, applied in your own voice. Your greetings and sign-offs stay, even when a generic rule would cut them.

  5. 5

    Audit Scan + judgment

    A pattern scan for AI tells, then a sentence-by-sentence read. The skill fixes every strong and moderate hit before you see the draft.

Draft

Ask it to write something or reply to something. It asks which methods to use, builds the piece, audits its own draft and hands it back ready to copy, with a two-line note naming the methods and format it used.

Audit

Paste a draft and ask whether it sounds like AI. You get a verdict, the structural problems, each tell with its fix, and a rewrite in your voice if the text is yours.

Things to ask it

  • Draft a note asking our contractor to push the deck start to 11/21.
  • Write a two-minute toast for my sister's wedding.
  • Does this complaint to the airline sound like AI?
  • What methods do you have?

Layer 5, on its own

Scan a draft for AI tells

This is the skill's pattern scanner, rebuilt to run in your browser. Paste a draft and it marks the phrases that make writing sound machine-made, with a fix for each one. Nothing you type leaves this page.

Inflation

Insisting something is important instead of showing it. Stands as a testament to, a vibrant community.

Vagueness

Gesturing at meaning without a claim. Experts say, a sentence that ends in highlighting something.

Mechanical rhythm

Patterns a reader feels before they can name them. It's not X, it's Y, em dashes, sentences all the same length.

Packaging

Formatting that looks like a chatbot answer. I hope this message finds you well, In summary, bold labels.

The scanner sees patterns, not meaning. After a clean scan, ask of every sentence: could it appear, unchanged, in a thousand other people's version of this piece? If yes, replace it with something only yours could say.

Methods

Five methods that sit on the pyramid

You pick the methods for each piece. Each one owns a single job (the opening, the grouping, the order, the edit or the numbers), which is why they stack. A piece built with four methods should be no longer than one built with Minto alone.

Pyramid Principle

Barbara Minto

Always on

What it does

One governing thought, written before anything else. Under it, two to four points of the same kind with no overlaps, then the specifics.

The test

If the reader stopped after the first sentence, would they know what you want or what you think?

SCQA

Barbara Minto

Controls the opening

Pick it for

Explaining a decision or a change, or a request that needs context before it makes sense.

What changes

The opening runs situation, complication, then your answer, in an order chosen for this reader.

MECE

Barbara Minto

Controls the grouping

Pick it for

Options, plans, comparisons, a list of asks: anything with categories.

What changes

Items sorted into clean groups on one dimension, with no overlaps and no gaps.

Inverted Pyramid

Newsroom practice

Controls the order

Pick it for

News, announcements, schedule changes and recaps of what happened.

What changes

A lede that answers who, what, when, where, why and how. Details follow in falling order of importance, so the reader can stop anywhere.

Writing Without Bullshit

Josh Bernoff

Controls the edit

Pick it for

Busy readers: a company, an office, a contractor. Also bad news, and replies in a thread that's gone long.

What changes

The first two sentences carry the message. Passive voice, jargon, weasel words and intensifiers come out, and the result is shorter.

Storytelling with Data

Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic

Controls the numbers

Pick it for

Anything with figures: budgets, cost comparisons, a dispute over an amount.

What changes

A one-sentence Big Idea. Only the numbers that matter, each with a comparison, and a small table only when the reader needs to look things up.

Formats

Where the point lands

The format comes from the request, so you don't have to pick one. When two fit, it picks the one whose reader has less time. Anything meant to be kept is a Letter, and anything spoken aloud is a Story.

The craft standard

Ten habits of an award-winning writer

Told to write like an award winner, a model reaches for adjectives and big claims, which is the first family of AI tells. So the skill defines the standard by what strong writers do.

  1. Find the detail only this piece has.

    Names, numbers, dates, what someone said. Without one, a piece can be correct and still forgettable.

  2. Let verbs do the work.

    "Fixed" beats "was able to resolve." Cut the adverbs propping up weak verbs.

  3. Make the first line earn the second.

    "Moving the deck start to 11/21?" beats "Following up."

  4. One idea per piece.

    If there are two, one leads and the other waits for a second message.

  5. Vary the rhythm on purpose.

    A short sentence after a long one. A colon, a question, a parenthesis when it fits.

  6. Earn the emotion.

    "She kept the voicemail for six years" says more than "it meant so much to her."

  7. Commit.

    Say "was the first" or "I think" when it's true. Hedging everything reads as nobody being home.

  8. Run the cover test.

    Hide the names. If it could be anyone's message to anyone, put the specifics back.

  9. Cut a fifth.

    The second pass removes about 20 percent: warm-up sentences, double adjectives, restatements.

  10. End strong.

    Close on the action, the date or the final image. Never on a recap or an offer of more help.
